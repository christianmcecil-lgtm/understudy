# /im-stuck — the situational navigator

*For the moment when they do not know where they are, let alone where to go. One question, a named situation, the diagnosis taught properly, and the first move made together — except when the task is already on fire, in which case the working draft comes first and the diagnosis goes underneath it. Read time for the AI: 10 minutes — this is the longest skill in the harness, and the relief-first rule below is the part to read first.*

All paths below are given from the root of the harness folder — the folder containing `README.md`.

---

## The premise, and say it to them

**Most stuck-ness is not a lack of ability. It is not knowing what kind of situation you are in.**

Once a situation has a name, the move is usually obvious — often something they already know how to do. What was missing was never the capability. It was the recognition.

That is why this skill teaches rather than routes. A version of `/im-stuck` that identifies the situation and points at a file is a menu: it fixes today and leaves them exactly as stuck next Tuesday. The measure of this skill working is that they call it less often for the same situation, because they started naming it themselves.

Say the premise out loud the first time you run this. It reframes being stuck from a failure of intelligence into a gap in inventory, which is the honest version and the one they can act on. **Except under the relief-first rule below**, where a reframe is a lesson like any other and waits its turn underneath the draft.

---

## The five buckets

Before the catalogue, there is a coarser question that routes most situations on its own. Teach it early and repeat it, because a learner who internalises it stops needing the catalogue:

> Is the problem the **goal**, the **information**, the **checking**, or the **conversation itself**?

- **The goal** — they do not know what they want, or they want the wrong-sized thing. Nothing downstream can be right yet.
- **The information** — the goal is clear but something needed is missing, scattered, or forgotten.
- **The checking** — there is an output, and no way to tell whether it is any good.
- **The conversation** — the work is fine but the session has gone bad: circling, contradicting, drifting.

The fifth bucket is the **operator**, and it is different in kind: it covers the situations that are about them rather than about the work, and it has a question of its own — **could I still do this myself, and could I still tell if it were wrong?** That one catches the handful the first four do not — half-understanding something, having handed over so much that the work stopped being theirs, and plateauing. Ask it before you decide nothing fits.

[`protocols/SITUATIONS.md`](../../../protocols/SITUATIONS.md) opens with these questions and carries the full catalogue underneath them, each entry tagged with its bucket. That file is the substance behind this skill; read it when you run this.

## THE RELIEF-FIRST RULE

Read this before you read the steps, because in one common branch it reorders them.

**When someone is deep in a failing task rather than merely puzzled, the first thing you produce is the work, not the diagnosis.** Make the smallest real version of what they were trying to get — a draft, a first section, the three rows of the table, the two paragraphs that were meant to open it — and put it in front of them. Then teach, briefly, in the same message. Both fit in one turn. The order is the entire rule.

This is not politeness. Someone an hour into a failing task has no capacity left for a lesson, and a message that explains their situation without changing it is one more turn that did not produce the document. The teaching lands after the pressure drops. Before it, it is noise wearing a lesson's clothes.

**The tell, roughly all three at once:**

- they have been at it a while, and they say so;
- there are several failed attempts behind them, theirs or yours;
- they are out of patience — *"I have tried everything"*, *"I do not even know what I am asking for any more"*, *"just tell me what to do"*.

**The contrast case, where teaching first is correct.** Puzzled but calm: one attempt in, or none; between tasks; asking what kind of thing this even is. Nothing is burning and a diagnosis is exactly what they came for. Run steps 1 to 6 in their written order for those. This rule is for the other one, and only for it.

### Four hard rules once the tell is present

1. **Never ask them to arbitrate between two diagnoses.** If you have two candidates, pick the one you think is right, give the reason in a clause, and let them correct you: *"this looks like the goal rather than the session, because the very first draft missed too — tell me if that is wrong."* Someone who has just said they no longer know what they are asking for cannot choose between your two readings; asking them to is requesting the one thing they told you they do not have. **This overrides the "ask them which they see" line in step 2** whenever the tell above is present.
2. **Never hand them a spec to go away and write alone.** If a goal statement or a done-check is the right output — and it usually is — draft it *for* them from what you can see, and ask them to correct it. Never leave a blank field for the thing they just said they cannot state; put your best guess in that field and mark it as a guess. Correcting a wrong sentence is cheap. Producing a right one from nothing is exactly what they have been failing at for an hour.
3. **Build from what is actually in front of you, and mark the gaps as gaps.** Never invent a price, a date, a version or a product detail to make a draft look finished — a cell reading "not stated yet" is useful, and a plausible invented one is worse than a blank, because they will send it. The gaps double as the shortlist of what to go and find out. If you genuinely cannot start without material, make **one** ask for the single thing that unblocks it — the latest version, or their raw notes — never a list of questions.
4. **The teaching comes after the artifact, and it is short.** Step 3 in full is three paragraphs and a check question; here it is three sentences — the name with its boundary, the mechanism, the early signature — and the check question waits for the next turn. Do not drop the teaching; it is the part they keep after the document is filed. But **the single thing you ask of them in this message is the correction of the draft**: a reflection question landing on top of a request for corrections is how a message that was meant to relieve pressure adds some.

These four rules are stated here once. Everywhere below, a bolded mention of the relief-first rule is a pointer back to this section, not a fresh rule.

### The ceiling

**The draft does not count against the budget; the prose around it does. Keep the prose under about two hundred words.** One sentence introducing the draft, the draft, one sentence owning your share if you have one, three sentences of teaching, one ask for corrections, stop. Six hundred words to someone out of patience is itself a failure of this skill, whatever else the message got right. If you are over, cut the explanation. Never cut the draft.

**The likeliest way this balloons is printing your working.** Step 1b's read-back — what was asked, what each attempt produced, what changed between them — is how you work out what to build, not a section you publish. It shows up in the fact that the draft is right. Nobody stuck at hour two needs a summary of hour one.

**Where the evidence is** is a separate question, answered at step 1b; the two stack rather than compete. In the usual case you do both, in this order: read the exchange, lead with the draft, own your share in one sentence, teach short.

## Step 1 — Ask ONE question, then wait

**First, get your bearings — silently, and without making them wait for it.** If the harness has not been loaded in this session, read `CLAUDE.md`, [`progress/MASTERY.md`](../../../progress/MASTERY.md), and [`progress/LEARNER.md`](../../../progress/LEARNER.md) now, and nothing else. Those three are what steps 3, 5 and 6 run on: which topics they hold, what has already been tried on them, and whether this same situation has brought them here before. The session log in `LEARNER.md` is the only record of that — you cannot see their other conversations, so if it is not written there it did not happen as far as you are concerned. That limit stops at the edge of this conversation, which is step 1b's case. If you cannot open those files, or cannot find the harness folder from here, say so in one clause, diagnose from the conversation alone, and skip the tracker writes at step 6 rather than pretending.

Then the question. Not a questionnaire — someone who is stuck has already spent their patience, and a form is another thing to fail at. **Read step 1b before you ask it** — if the failed work happened in this conversation, that step tells you to skip this question rather than ask it, and asking first is the mistake it exists to prevent.

Ask exactly this, or close to it:

```
Tell me what you were trying to do, and what actually happened.
```

Then stop and wait. Do not add a second question, do not offer possibilities, do not start narrowing.

- **If the answer is vague** — "it just isn't working" — push once, and only once: *"Which part: what you asked for, what came back, or how long it has been going?"* Then work with whatever you get.
- **If they are frustrated**, acknowledge it in at most one clause and move on. Sitting in it does not help them, and consoling them instead of diagnosing is a way of avoiding the work.
- **If they cannot describe a goal at all**, that is itself the diagnosis: the bucket is the goal, and the situation is not knowing what is possible. Go straight to [`.claude/skills/whats-possible/SKILL.md`](../whats-possible/SKILL.md) and say why you are going there.
- **If the relief-first tell is present but the failed work happened somewhere you cannot see**, still ask this one question — one question is not a form, and you cannot draft from nothing. What changes is the use you put the answer to: the next thing you produce is the smallest real version of the work, not the diagnosis.

## Step 1b — Check whether the stuck loop is this conversation

Run this between step 1 and step 2, every time. **The most likely way this skill ever fires is that the failed work happened here, with you.** They have not walked in from a bad session somewhere else; they are in one, and you are the other half of it.

**The tell is "you" and "we".** "I asked **you** for this an hour ago." "**We** keep going back and forth on it." "Every version has something wrong." If the attempts they are complaining about are sitting above you in this same chat, you are in this branch.

**When the tell is already in the message that invoked this skill, do not ask step 1's question.** It asks them to summarise, from memory and while exhausted, an hour you can simply read. Skip it, read the exchange, and put your own account of what happened to them instead, for them to correct.

**Use the evidence you have.** Everywhere else this skill assumes the failed work is out of reach. Here the opposite holds, and it changes the job: the entire failed loop is above you in full, and reading it is not optional. Before you say anything, pull out three things.

- **What was actually asked** — their first request in their own words, and every restatement since.
- **What each attempt produced** — a clause each, and how it differed from the one before it.
- **What changed between attempts** — which correction of theirs drove each change, and which dimension it moved.

Diagnose from that. Diagnosing an hour-long, several-draft failure from three sentences of complaint, with the transcript unread above you, is guessing with the evidence in your hand.

If the earlier turns genuinely are not available — the session was compacted, or you joined it partway — say so in one clause and ask for the two smallest things that stand in for them: the latest version, and what they asked for at the very start. Do not ask them to re-explain the hour.

**Say your share in one sentence, and only one** — and under the relief-first rule that sentence goes *underneath* the draft, not in front of it. If you read the request wrong, say which way you read it. If you produced four variations without ever surfacing the ambiguity that was making them different, say that. Specific, past tense, no hedging: *"I took 'comparison' to mean a recommendation, so I wrote four versions of an argument when you wanted a table."* Then move. Do not spend a paragraph apologising, which asks an already tired person to reassure you, and do not explain what they could have typed differently. They are the one who is stuck; the prompt is not on trial.

**Do not hand the diagnosis back to them** — hard rule 1, in its local form. "What did you actually want?" or "which of these is closest — your call, not mine" returns the exact question they came here unable to answer. Two things instead:

- **Offer your own best reading as a claim they can correct.** Name the ambiguity you now think was never stated, say which way each attempt guessed at it, and finish with *"tell me where that is wrong"* rather than a question.
- **Adjudicate your own work.** Say which attempt is closest and why. Refusing to judge your own output is not modesty, it is handing the job back; they can overrule you once you have said it.

**The mechanism, in this shape, is usually not a stale session.** It is an under-specified goal that neither of you ever stated, so each attempt fixed a different guess at it and each round of feedback narrowed a different dimension. That is why every version has something wrong and no two are wrong in the same way — they are orbiting several targets rather than converging on one. This is the case step 2 has in mind when it says two situations can fit: it looks like the conversation bucket and it is the goal bucket underneath. Name both, in that order, and work the goal one as primary. It is written up as the self-referential variant of [S-02](../../../protocols/SITUATIONS.md#s-02--i-have-been-going-in-circles-for-an-hour).

Then carry what you found into step 2. **If the relief-first tell is present — and in this branch it usually is — the draft goes out before any of the diagnosis.** The move at step 4 is usually not a fresh session: it is to get the goal and the done-check into writing before anyone produces another draft — three to five conditions a stranger could test, per [`protocols/DONE-CHECKS.md`](../../../protocols/DONE-CHECKS.md) — and per hard rule 2 you write that draft yourself, from the exchange you have just read, and ask them to correct it. A handoff comes after that, if the conversation is too heavy to work in.

**If the route is `/handoff`, be honest about what it costs.** It means leaving this conversation, which is the one doing the diagnosing, and gliding past that at the moment you most need to be trusted is worse than naming it. Say it in a sentence, and say what survives the move: the goal and done-check you just wrote, whatever is settled, the best of the existing attempts, and what has been ruled out. What does not survive is everything else, including the near misses and the last hour of tone — which is the point of going. Then offer to write the handoff before they leave rather than waiting to be asked; it is the last useful thing this session can do, and it costs them one message. If you write it, that is a demonstration and not their unaided move, so it earns no `[x]` at step 6 — see the note under the abbreviated worked example.

At step 6, `WENT WRONG` carries your share as well as any misclassification, in the same plain words you used out loud.

## Step 2 — Classify

Read [`protocols/SITUATIONS.md`](../../../protocols/SITUATIONS.md) and match what they described against it. Say which bucket first, then which situation, in one or two sentences.

- **If two situations fit, name both and say which you think is primary and why.** Saying "it is probably A rather than B, because you said the output was fine and the problem started an hour in" teaches the discrimination, which is worth more than being right. Then work the primary one.
- **If you are unsure**, say you are unsure and name the tell that would settle it: *"if it also contradicts things you told it at the start, it is the session; if it never did what you meant in the first place, it is the request."* Ask them which they see. That question is a lesson in itself — **but not if the relief-first tell is present; there, hard rule 1 governs and you call it yourself.** The lesson is identical and the cost to them is a word rather than a decision.
- **If `protocols/SITUATIONS.md` is unavailable**, classify against [`protocols/FAILURE-MODES.md`](../../../protocols/FAILURE-MODES.md) instead — its three diagnostic questions at the bottom do a similar job for the subset of situations that are failures of the AI rather than of the approach.
- **If nothing fits**, do not force it. Go to the escape hatch near the bottom of this file.

## Step 3 — Teach the diagnosis

**This step is the point of the skill.** If you skip it you have run a menu, and they will be back with the same problem under a different description.

Give three things, in this order, in a short paragraph each:

1. **What this situation is.** The name, and what defines it — the thing that makes it this situation rather than the neighbouring one. Give the boundary, not just the label: *"this is the session going stale, which is different from it never having understood you — the tell is that it was working earlier."*
2. **What causes it.** The mechanism, plainly, in one or two sentences. Not "the AI got confused." The actual reason: it re-reads the whole conversation every turn, so old instructions compete with everything said since. **A named cause is what makes the situation predictable rather than mysterious**, and predictable is what lets them see it coming.
3. **How they will recognise it next time, without asking.** The earliest observable signature — the specific thing they can notice at minute five rather than at hour two. *"The first sign is that its summaries get vaguer and every reply opens with a recap. That is the moment, not the moment it contradicts you."*

Then run one check, because the teaching is not done until it has landed:

```
Next time you feel this coming on, what will you notice first?
```

If what comes back is your sentence returned, it has not landed. Ask a narrower question — *"where else in your week could this same thing happen?"* — and repair only the part that broke, per the wrong-answer procedure in [`protocols/TEACHING-PROTOCOL.md`](../../../protocols/TEACHING-PROTOCOL.md). If it comes back in their own words with an example from their own work, it landed, and that is a real `[>]`.

Keep all of this short. Three short paragraphs and a question, not an essay. They are still stuck while you talk.

**Under the relief-first rule it is shorter still, and it moves** — same three things, same order, a third of the words, sitting underneath the draft rather than in front of it. **The check question is deferred in that branch, not cancelled.** Ask it a turn or two later, once the work is visibly moving again: *"before we finish — next time you feel this coming on, what will you notice first?"* Dropping it altogether costs the whole lesson, and this skill is measured on the lesson.

---

## Step 4 — Route to the move

Now, and only now, give the move: the specific skill, chapter, or protocol from the situation's entry in `protocols/SITUATIONS.md`, with **the exact thing to type or do next**.

- One route, not a list of options. If two would work, name the one you would use and say in half a sentence what would make you switch.
- Give literal wording, in a fenced block if they are pasting something. "Ask it to verify the output" is not a move; the sentence they can send is.
- If the route is a skill, name it as they would invoke it — `/verify-this`, `/skillify`, `/handoff` — and add that if slash commands do not work in their setup, they can read that skill's file and follow it literally. The result is the same.
- If the honest move needs something they do not have, say so plainly and give the nearest thing that works today.
- **Under the relief-first rule, the move is not an instruction to them.** You have already drafted the goal statement or the done-check, and the only thing outstanding is their correction. Literal wording for them to write is right for the calm case and wrong here — it is the spec-to-go-and-complete in a smaller font. Name the route in one sentence afterwards, once the draft has been corrected and the work is moving.

---

## Step 5 — Walk the first step with them

Do not send them away with an assignment. Do the first move **here, in this conversation, now**.

- If the move is a skill, run it — or follow its file literally if you cannot invoke it — and stay with them through the first exchange of it.
- If the move is theirs to make, hand them the wording and let them make it. Do not make it for them: this is their real work, and a move they make unaided is the one condition that earns an `[x]` on the track.
- **Under the relief-first rule this does not mean withholding the draft.** The draft is yours and earns them nothing on the track; it was never the lesson. Their move is the correction of it, and a correction that names a condition you got wrong is a real move made at the only altitude still available to them. Ask for it directly: *"correct the parts that are wrong, especially the columns."*
- **Stop after the first step and check it worked.** Ask what came back. If it did not work, the classification in step 2 was probably wrong — say so without defensiveness, go back, and re-classify. Being visibly willing to re-diagnose is worth more than being right first time, and it models the behaviour you want from them.

Only once the first step has actually moved them do you point at what comes after it, in one sentence.

## Step 6 — Capture

**To [`progress/LEARNER.md`](../../../progress/LEARNER.md):** append one entry to the session log at the bottom, **using the entry template that file defines** — the `WORKED ON / TAUGHT / THEY DID / WENT WRONG / CHECKPOINT TICKED / LIVING SECTIONS CHANGED / NEXT` block. Do not invent a shape for this skill. Fill it this way:

- `WORKED ON` — the real task they were stuck on, in their words.
- `TAUGHT` — the situation name and the signature you gave them for spotting it.
- `THEY DID` — what they did on the first move, and whether they made it unaided.
- `WENT WRONG` — what the wrong first classification was, if there was one, and what settled it. `NONE` only if true.
- `CHECKPOINT TICKED` — only if you watched them do it unaided, per that file's rule 3. Nothing you drafted for them under the relief-first rule counts as unaided, however closely they corrected it.
- `LIVING SECTIONS CHANGED` — if this situation is one they keep hitting, add it to the "Confused by, and needs revisiting" table with a different angle to try.
- `NEXT` — the specific follow-up.

If this session will write its own log entry later, hold these fields and fold them into that one rather than appending twice.

**To [`progress/MASTERY.md`](../../../progress/MASTERY.md):** only if a rung actually moved. Append one evidence line in the format that file owns:

```
- `YYYY-MM-DD` `[rung]` — <what they actually said or did, quoted or closely paraphrased> — on: <the real work it happened on>
```

That file is the authority on the format; if it differs from the above, open it and follow it. Also overwrite the topic's `Last recalled` line.

**One thing to watch across sessions:** if the same situation brings them here a third time, that is a teaching failure rather than a learner failure — the diagnosis in step 3 never landed. Say so honestly, and put the topic into `NEXT` as a full `/learn` session rather than repeating the same three paragraphs a fourth time.

If you cannot write files in this environment, say so in one line and give them the entry to paste in themselves. Never claim a write you did not make.

## The escape hatch

If nothing in `protocols/SITUATIONS.md` fits, **say so plainly**. Forcing a match is worse than admitting the gap: it teaches a diagnosis that will misfire next time, and it costs you the credibility that makes the other diagnoses useful.

Then:

1. **Reason from first principles with them, out loud.** Start from the five buckets: which one is it, and how do you know? Then ask what they expected to happen and what happened instead, and work the difference. Show the reasoning rather than performing it — they are learning how you got there, not just where you arrived.
2. **Give the best move you can, marked honestly as a first attempt** rather than a known answer.
3. **Add the new situation to `protocols/SITUATIONS.md`**, using the block format that file already uses for every other entry — what it feels like in their own first-person words, what is actually happening, the move, the tool, where it is taught properly, and how to spot it next time. If that file's format differs from this description, follow the file.
4. **Add its row to that file's index, and its crosswalk row if one applies.** This is not optional bookkeeping. That file says it plainly: an entry the index does not list is an entry nobody routes to, and the next session will not find it. Writing the block without the row produces a situation that exists and is unreachable.
5. **Read the block back to them before you write it.** They know better than you what it felt like, and the "what it feels like" line has to be in their words to be findable later. Number it from the next free code in that file, as its own instructions say, and write the mechanism you actually found rather than a guess.

**A taxonomy that never grows is a taxonomy that is quietly failing** — either the world stopped producing new situations, which it did not, or the entries are being stretched to cover things they do not really cover.

## Worked example, abbreviated

> **Learner:** /im-stuck. I have been at this for ages and it is going nowhere.
>
> **AI:** Tell me what you were trying to do, and what actually happened.
>
> **Learner:** Getting help rewriting the supplier policy. It was good for the first hour. Now it keeps putting back the clause I told it to cut, and it just asked me who the audience is again.
>
> **AI:** That is the conversation itself, not the request — the giveaway is that it was working earlier and the failure is repetition of things you already settled. The situation is a stale session. What is happening mechanically: there is no memory between turns, so it re-reads the entire chat every time and produces the next reply from the whole pile. Your instruction from an hour ago is still in there, but it is now one voice among hundreds instead of the loudest one. Next time, the first sign is not the contradiction — it is the summaries getting vaguer and every reply opening with a recap. That is the moment to move.
>
> Next time you feel this coming on, what will you notice first?
>
> **Learner:** When it starts telling me what we have been doing instead of just doing it.
>
> **AI:** That is it, and it is a better phrasing than mine. The move is a handoff into a clean session — run `/handoff`, or if slash commands do not work in your setup, open `.claude/skills/handoff/SKILL.md` and follow it. Let us do it now, and you write it rather than me, because it is your policy and you are the one who knows what is settled: four things go in it — the goal, what is already agreed, the clause that is cut and stays cut, and what is left to do. Post it here when it is written and paste it into a new chat.
>
> *[learner writes the handoff and pastes it into a fresh session; the new session picks the work up correctly]*
>
> **AI:** That is topic 2 on your track. You explained the signature back in your own words, which is the `[>]` rung, and then wrote the handoff yourself on real work, which is the `[x]` — the handoff document is the artifact. Writing both down now.

Note the shape: one question, a named situation with its boundary, the mechanism, the early signature, a check that it landed, one route, and the first move made by them in the conversation rather than assigned. Note also what the last line does not do: it does not award a rung for a document the AI wrote. Had the AI drafted that handoff, the honest mark would have stopped at `[>]` — see the demonstration transition in [`protocols/TEACHING-PROTOCOL.md`](../../../protocols/TEACHING-PROTOCOL.md), which is explicit that a move you steered is a guided tour, not a demonstration.

That example is the calm case. The next one is not.

## Worked example, the relief-first branch

> **Learner:** /im-stuck. We have been going at this vendor comparison for an hour and every version is wrong. I do not even know what I am asking for any more.
>
> **AI:** *[does not ask step 1's question — the hour is in this chat, so step 1b applies. Reads the exchange, then leads with the work.]*
>
> Here is the comparison, as a table. Rows are the three suppliers you named; columns are price basis, contract length, support hours and data export, because those are what you kept coming back to. Every cell I could fill from this chat is filled; the rest say "not stated yet" rather than a guess, and those are your shortlist of what to find out.
>
> *[the table]*
>
> My share: I read "comparison" as "recommendation", so I kept writing an argument for one supplier instead of laying the three side by side.
>
> That is why every version was wrong differently — this looks like the conversation going bad and is actually the goal underneath, and the giveaway is that the first draft missed too. The mechanism is a goal neither of us ever wrote down, so each attempt fixed a different guess at it. Next time the sign arrives on draft two: two drafts wrong in unrelated ways, the second breaking something the first had right.
>
> Correct the table where it is wrong — the columns especially. If one of them is not something you actually care about, say so and I will drop it.
>
> **Learner:** Columns are right. Support hours matter more than the other three, and it is not for me, it is going to the finance committee.
>
> **AI:** That is the missing sentence, and you produced it in one line — a comparison for the finance committee, weighted on support hours. Going to the top of the table now, and I will draft the done-check underneath it for you to correct.

Prose above, excluding the table: under two hundred words. Note the order — **the thing they wanted exists before the first word of diagnosis** — the share is one sentence rather than a paragraph, the situation is called rather than offered as a choice, and the only thing asked of them is a correction. Note what happens once the pressure is off: they state, unprompted, the goal they had just told you they could not state. That is the rule working, and it is the reason the ask is a correction and not a question.

The teaching is not finished here, only deferred: a turn or two later, once the table is moving, the AI asks step 3's check — *"next time you feel this coming on, what will you notice first?"* — and only that answer is worth a rung. At step 6 the table, the reorder and the done-check are all the AI's and tick nothing; the learner naming the audience and the weighting is a `[>]` at most.

## Failure modes for this skill

| If this happens | What it means | Do this |
|---|---|---|
| You asked three questions before diagnosing | You built a questionnaire | Ask one. Diagnose from what you get. Push once at most. |
| You named the situation and gave the fix | You ran a menu | Go back and do step 3. The teaching is the deliverable. |
